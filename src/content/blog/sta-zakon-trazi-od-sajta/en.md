Short answer: a small business website in Serbia should ask for consent before it sets tracking cookies, explain how it processes visitors’ data and show the company’s details. An online shop also needs terms of sale and a notice on out-of-court resolution of consumer disputes; since 2 August 2026, that notice has been explicitly required on the website as well. I checked 110 small business websites: on 83 of them (75%), tracking cookies are stored before the visitor makes any choice, and in 39 of 49 online shops (80%), I didn’t find a notice on the pages I reviewed saying that the trader is legally obliged to take part in out-of-court dispute resolution. The good news is that all of this can be fixed with changes to the website, and the Electronic Communications Act (ZEK) doesn’t impose a fine for breaking the cookie rules alone: a fine only follows if the business fails to comply with an inspector’s order.

The results are anonymous and aggregated: I don’t publish the names of the websites and I don’t report businesses to the inspectorate, because I’m on the side of businesses that want to comply.

## How I checked

I build websites for small businesses and have developed a scanner, a tool that goes through a website the way an ordinary visitor would. Through searches on 22 October 2026, I selected 112 websites: 64 small online shops (from jewellery and natural cosmetics to coffee and toys) and 48 private practices and clinics (dentists in Belgrade, Novi Sad, Niš and Kragujevac, gynaecology practices and polyclinics). The sample isn’t representative of all websites in Serbia, but it shows what is common among small businesses.

I scanned them on 5 November 2026. The tool opens each website three times: with no choice made, after clicking “Reject” and after clicking “Accept”. It records tracking services and cookies, looks for a cookie banner, and reads the homepage and the legal pages that links lead to. It doesn’t log in to any account, fill in forms or buy anything. Two websites blocked it with bot protection, so 110 scans were completed.

While developing the tool, I checked its findings by hand and kept refining it, because the information was sometimes under a different name or in an unexpected place, such as a page called “Returns” instead of “Complaints”. If the scanner hasn’t actually read a page (a PDF, a page that didn’t load, terms that only appear at the payment step), I don’t claim that anything is missing from it. And since it doesn’t read every page of a website either, I write “I didn’t find”, not “there isn’t”. I’d rather miss a finding than tell you something is missing when you actually have it.

## Cookies: tracking starts before the visitor chooses anything

On 83 of the 110 websites (75%), cookies from tracking services are stored in the visitor’s browser as soon as the page opens. The services most often loaded before any choice are Google Analytics (78 sites), Google Ads (51), Meta Pixel (48) and Microsoft Clarity (9), which records how visitors move around the page.

Cookies are set before any choice by 51 of the 62 scanned online shops (82%) and by 32 of the 48 practices (67%).

**What the law requires.** Article 160(4) of the [Electronic Communications Act](https://pravno-informacioni-sistem.rs/eli/rep/sgrs/skupstina/zakon/2023/35/1/reg) (ZEK) allows cookies only with the user’s consent, after clear and complete information about their purpose and with the option to refuse them. Analytics and advertising cookies don’t fall under the exception in paragraph 5, which covers only what serves solely to transmit a communication or to provide a service the user has explicitly requested.

## Cookie banners: rarely found, and even more rarely working properly

Of the 110 websites, 89 use some kind of tracking service, and on 67 of those I found no cookie banner at all (among practices, on 30 of the 33 sites with tracking). I found a banner on 23 of all 110 websites:

- on 16, I didn’t find a “Reject” button on the first screen;
- on 6, tracking continues even after clicking “Reject”;
- on only one, “Reject” works properly and tracking doesn’t start.

So having a banner doesn’t necessarily mean it’s doing its job, and Article 160(4) also requires that visitors be able to refuse. Check yours with the test below.

## Online shops: the notice on out-of-court dispute resolution

The scanner recognised a shopping basket or a well-known e-commerce platform on 50 of the 62 scanned shops, so I count this finding only for those. For one of them, the pages with the terms didn’t load, so of the remaining 49:

- in 39 (80%), I didn’t find a notice on the pages I reviewed (homepage, terms of sale, complaints, delivery, privacy policy, contact) saying that the trader is legally obliged to take part in out-of-court resolution of consumer disputes. Of those 39, 37 don’t mention out-of-court resolution on those pages at all, and 2 mention it only as an option for the customer, without saying that the trader is obliged to take part;
- 10 have the notice.

**What the law requires.** The new [Consumer Protection Act](https://www.parlament.gov.rs/upload/archive/files/lat/pdf/zakoni/14_saziv/1317-26-lat..pdf) (ZZP, Official Gazette 35/2026) has applied since 2 August 2026. Article 173(3) requires the trader to display a notice at the point of sale and on its website, in a way that is clear, understandable and easily accessible, stating that it is legally obliged to take part in out-of-court consumer dispute resolution. The obligation to take part has existed since March 2022; what’s new is that the notice is now explicitly required on the website too, and that the fines are higher.

**What this means for you.** In this procedure, a dispute with a customer is handled not by a court but by a body on the list kept by the ministry responsible for consumer protection. A customer can only start it after first making a complaint or objection to you (Article 173(1)), so the first conversation still goes through you. Look at your footer and terms of sale: if they only say that the customer has this option, that isn’t the notice the law requires. They need to say that you are legally obliged to take part. A suggested sentence for both places, in Serbian, with an English translation below:

> “Trgovac [naziv firme] je po zakonu obavezan da učestvuje u postupku vansudskog rešavanja potrošačkih sporova.”
>
> “The trader [company name] is legally obliged to take part in out-of-court consumer dispute resolution proceedings.”

## How big is the real risk?

An overview of the obligations and fines (ZZPL is the Personal Data Protection Act and ZET the E-Commerce Act):

| Obligation | Law | Fine set by law (RSD) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cookies | ZEK, Article 169(1)(49) | the ZEK sets no fine for breaking the cookie rules alone; only if the business fails to comply with an inspector’s order: legal entity 500,000 to 2,000,000, sole trader 50,000 to 500,000 |
| Privacy policy | ZZPL, Article 95(1)(8) | legal entity 50,000 to 2,000,000, responsible person 5,000 to 150,000, sole trader 20,000 to 500,000 |
| Company details | ZET, Article 22 | legal entity 100,000, responsible person 10,000, sole trader 20,000 |
| Pre-purchase information | ZZP, Article 210(1)(3) and (8) | legal entity 200,000, responsible person 50,000, sole trader 100,000 |
| Out-of-court dispute resolution | ZZP, Article 210(1)(35) | legal entity 200,000, responsible person 50,000, sole trader 100,000 |

And here’s how it looks in practice:

- **For cookies**, a fine under the ZZPL is possible, but that’s an interpretation not yet confirmed in practice: I found no published fine or other measure for it from 2019 to October 2026, which doesn’t mean there definitely aren’t any.
- **The Commissioner** (Poverenik), who oversees how the ZZPL is applied, completed 1,169 inspections in 2025, issued 101 warnings and filed only 3 requests with misdemeanour courts ([annual report for 2025](https://poverenik.rs/h-content/uploads/2026/03/godisnji-izvestaj-2025-poverenik.pdf)). The fines courts have imposed on companies since 2020 have mostly been between RSD 50,000 and 100,000.
- **The market inspectorate** has e-commerce checks in its annual plan for 2026, including the information customers get before buying through a website.
- **A first breach** carrying a fixed fine of up to RSD 200,000 (in the table, these are the amounts without a range), corrected within the deadline the inspector sets, is as a rule not penalised (Inspection Supervision Act, Article 42(3)). This doesn’t apply to repeat offences.

That’s no reason to wait, but it’s no reason to panic either.

## How to check your own website

The cookie test takes about five minutes:

1. Open the website in a private (incognito) window and don’t touch the banner.
2. On a computer, press F12 in Chrome (Cmd + Option + I on a Mac), open “Application” → “Cookies” and select the website’s address.
3. If you already see `_ga`, `_gid`, `_fbp`, `_gcl_au` or similar cookies, tracking starts without consent.
4. Close all private windows, open the website in a new private window, click “Reject” and look at the list again. If tracking cookies still appear, “Reject” isn’t working properly. If there’s no “Reject” button on the first screen, that needs fixing too.

Then look at the footer: it should show the company name, registered office address, registration number (matični broj), tax ID (PIB) and email. Also check whether your privacy policy names the services whose cookies you saw (`_ga` is set by Google Analytics and `_fbp` by Meta Pixel).

## How to fix it

- **Cookies:** a cookie banner that blocks analytics and advertising scripts until the visitor clicks “Accept”, with a “Reject” button on the first screen, and for Google services, Consent Mode v2 with the default state set to “denied” (whoever maintains your website sets this up). After the change, run the test again.
- **Privacy policy** ([ZZPL](https://www.paragraf.rs/propisi/zakon_o_zastiti_podataka_o_licnosti.html), Article 23): one that names the services the website actually uses, such as Google Analytics and Meta Pixel.
- **Contact form:** a short note on data processing below the button, with a link to the privacy policy.
- **Footer:** company name, registered office address, registration number, tax ID and email ([ZET](https://www.paragraf.rs/propisi/zakon_o_elektronskoj_trgovini.html), Article 6).
- **Online shop:** pages covering the terms of sale, complaints, delivery and the right to withdraw within 14 days (with a withdrawal form), in Serbian (ZZP, Articles 12 and 27), linked from the footer and from the ordering process. The sentence on out-of-court dispute resolution goes in the footer and in the terms of sale.

If you run an online shop, I’d start with the pre-purchase information and the out-of-court dispute resolution notice.

## FAQ

**Do I need a cookie banner if I only use Google Analytics?**
Yes. Analytics cookies aren’t covered by the exception in Article 160(5) of the ZEK, so you need consent for them, and a banner is the usual way to ask for it.

**Do I need a privacy policy if I don’t have a contact form?**
If your website uses analytics, yes. The ZZPL (Article 23) requires you to inform visitors when you collect their data, and it isn’t only contact forms that collect data: analytics does too.

**I run a private practice, not an online shop. What applies to me?**
The same rules as for any website with analytics or a contact form: cookies, a privacy policy and company details. The law exempts medical treatment services from the rules on out-of-court consumer dispute resolution (Article 171(4) of the ZZP).

This article isn’t legal advice: if you’re unsure how a rule applies to your business, ask a lawyer.
